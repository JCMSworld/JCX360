Regulatory compliance to Data engineering:
What banking data programmes really require?

An experience-driven view of how banking regulatory requirements translate into data, architecture, engineering, governance and delivery.

Published on October 2, 2026 by JCX360

Overview

Banking regulatory programmes are often seen as compliance exercises. In large technology programmes, I have found that the reality is much broader.

They are complex data and technology transformation programmes where the bank needs to make sure that data is accurate, complete, traceable, secure and available at the right time.

Regulations such as BCBS239, IFRS 9, Basel IV, SRB and DORA create requirements around risk data, financial reporting, operational resilience and governance. Meeting those requirements often means bringing together data from platforms such as Murex 3.0, Athena, SAP, CRM and nCino, along with data integration, data quality, lineage, reconciliation and reporting capabilities.

The technology stack can include Informatica, Teradata, Databricks, Tableau and AxiomSL, with modern data platforms increasingly using layered approaches such as Bronze, Silver and Gold.

The important point is that regulatory reporting is only as reliable as the data and controls behind it.

For a Data Engineering Manager or Technology Delivery Leader, the job is therefore not simply to deliver a reporting solution. It is to connect the regulatory requirement to the architecture, engineering, governance and delivery model, and make sure that the final numbers can be explained, reconciled and audited.

My experience leading large-scale data programmes

Over the years, I have led large-scale banking data and technology programmes across BCBS239, Murex 3.0, Athena, IFRS 9, Basel IV, nCino and SRB, working across data engineering, architecture, risk, finance, compliance and technology teams.

The scale of the role included 100+ engineers, 10+ Scrum teams and 20+ programmes running in parallel, with multiple stakeholders, competing priorities and continuous changes in delivery demand.

My experience has shown me that regulatory compliance is fundamentally a data and technology challenge. The complexity is not only understanding the regulation, but establishing where the data comes from, which source is authoritative, how it is transformed, whether it is accurate and complete, and whether the final reported number can be traced, reconciled and audited.

At this scale, delivery also becomes a capacity, dependency and stakeholder management challenge. Priority changes, specialist skill constraints, upstream and downstream dependencies, shared technology platforms and competing programme demands can directly affect delivery outcomes. Managing these dependencies and influencing teams outside the immediate delivery structure becomes as important as managing the engineering teams themselves.

Working across platforms including Teradata, Informatica, Tableau, Murex 3.0, Athena, SAP and nCino, I focused on delivering reliable data flows and regulatory outcomes across a complex banking technology landscape.


This article below shares some of the practical lessons from that experience, covering data lineage, data quality, architecture, regulatory reporting, security, governance and large-scale delivery.

It starts with the data

When people hear about banking regulations such as BCBS239, IFRS 9, Basel IV, SRB or DORA, the first thing that often comes to mind is compliance.

In large technology programmes, I have found that the bigger challenge is usually the data behind the compliance requirement.

A regulation may define what the bank needs to achieve, but the technology organisation has to make sure the underlying data is available, accurate, complete, traceable, secure and delivered on time.

That is where things start becoming complicated.

A typical banking environment does not have one system containing everything required for regulatory reporting.

Data can come from many different platforms.

Core banking systems provide accounts, loans and transactions.

CRM platforms provide customer and interaction data.

Lending platforms such as nCino provide loan origination, credit and workflow information.

Trading platforms such as Murex 3.0 and Athena provide trade, valuation, pricing and risk information.

SAP provides important finance and accounting data.

Digital channels generate additional customer and transaction information.

Each of these systems can have different data models, identifiers, formats, interfaces and ownership models.

So the challenge is not simply moving the data from one system to another.

The bigger question is whether the data still means the same thing when it reaches the regulatory reporting process.

That is where data engineering becomes important.

BCBS239 makes lineage critical

One of the important lessons from BCBS239 is understanding where a number actually comes from.

Imagine a senior risk report showing the bank's total credit exposure.

The obvious question is:

Where did that number come from?

A good regulatory data platform should allow the organisation to trace the number backwards.

Regulatory report → Gold dataset → Transformation → Silver dataset → Bronze dataset → Source system → Original transaction

That is data lineage.

If the number cannot be traced back to its source, investigated and reproduced, there is a problem.

For me, lineage is therefore much more than a technical diagram.

It becomes part of the control framework.

When someone asks why a number changed, the organisation needs to be able to follow the data through the platform and understand what happened.

That is particularly important in regulatory environments where the answer cannot simply be, "the system produced this number".

Data quality is more than checking for nulls

Data quality is sometimes treated as a simple validation exercise.

In a regulatory environment, it is much broader than checking whether a field is populated.

The organisation needs to know:

  • Is the data complete?
  • Is it accurate?
  • Is it consistent?
  • Is it available on time?
  • Is it coming from the correct source?
  • Has it been transformed correctly?
  • Can the result be reconciled?
  • Is the data owner clearly identified?
  • Can an exception be investigated?

For example, a customer account may exist in one system but have a different identifier in another.

A trade may exist in Murex but not appear correctly in a downstream risk dataset.

A finance balance may not reconcile with the regulatory dataset.

These may look like technical defects when they first appear, but the impact can go much further.

If the underlying data is wrong, the regulatory reporting can be wrong as well.

This is why data quality needs clear ownership, controls and remediation processes rather than simply a set of validation rules.

Where Murex 3.0 and Athena fit

Trading and market risk platforms introduce another level of complexity.

Murex 3.0 can provide trade, valuation, market data and risk information.

Athena can provide trading, pricing, P&L and risk-related information.

The downstream data platform needs to understand these feeds and preserve their context.

Murex / Athena → Raw data ingestion → Standardised risk data →Curated risk datasets →Risk reporting / regulatory reporting

But the important part is not just moving the data through those layers.

The question I would want to be able to answer is:

Which trade, valuation date, market data and transformation produced this regulatory number?

That is where engineering, data governance and regulatory requirements come together.

IFRS 9 is also a data problem

IFRS 9 is another good example.

Expected Credit Loss calculations depend on data and models involving concepts such as:

  • Probability of Default (PD)
  • Loss Given Default (LGD)
  • Exposure At Default (EAD)
  • Staging
  • Expected Credit Loss (ECL)

From a technology perspective, the challenge is making sure the required customer, product, exposure and historical data is available, controlled and traceable.

The result also needs to reconcile with financial reporting.

So the engineering question becomes:

Can we trace the ECL number from the financial output back through the model inputs and ultimately to the source data?

There is a significant difference between producing a number and producing a number that can be explained and audited.

That distinction becomes very important in regulatory programmes.

Basel IV adds another layer

Basel requirements introduce additional complexity around capital and risk calculations.

From a data engineering perspective, this means providing reliable datasets for areas such as:

  • Exposure
  • Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)
  • Capital
  • Liquidity
  • LCR
  • NSFR

Again, the technology challenge is not simply calculating the metric.

The organisation needs confidence that the underlying data is complete, correctly transformed and reconciled.

If the calculation is correct but the input data is wrong, the outcome is still wrong.

That is why the data controls around the calculation are just as important as the calculation itself.

SRB and resolution data

Resolution reporting introduces another important requirement.

The Single Resolution Board (SRB) needs information about the bank's financial position, liabilities, legal entities and other information required for resolution planning.

This creates another requirement for controlled and reconciled data.

This is where the concept of a Golden Copy becomes particularly useful.

Different systems can contain different versions of information.

The organisation needs to establish which data is authoritative for a particular reporting purpose and make sure that downstream reporting uses the controlled version.

This does not necessarily mean there is one system that owns every piece of data.

It means there is a clear understanding of which version should be used for a particular business or regulatory purpose.

The Medallion architecture approach

Modern data platforms often use a layered architecture to manage this complexity.

Bronze

This is the raw source data.

The priority here is preservation and traceability.

Source information, ingestion timestamps and technical metadata are retained so that the original data can be understood later.

Silver

This is where the data becomes standardised and quality controlled.

Transformation, validation, standardisation, deduplication and data quality controls become important at this stage.

Gold

This is where business and regulatory-ready datasets are created.

These datasets can support risk reporting, finance, regulatory reporting and analytical use cases.

But the architecture itself does not make a platform compliant.

The controls around the architecture are what matter.

You can have a very modern data platform, but if you cannot explain where the data came from, who owns it, how it was transformed or whether it reconciles, the architecture alone does not solve the regulatory problem.

The downstream side matters too

One thing that can easily happen in large data programmes is a strong focus on upstream ingestion while the downstream consumers receive less attention.

Regulatory data can ultimately feed platforms such as:

  • SAP
  • Tableau
  • Regulatory reporting platforms
  • Risk engines
  • Finance systems
  • Management reporting

For example, a regulatory reporting platform such as AxiomSL may consume controlled and validated data from upstream banking systems and data platforms.

Source systems → Data platform → Regulatory data → Reporting platform → Regulatory submission

Every transition introduces another opportunity for a data quality, reconciliation, security or lineage issue.

That is why I prefer to look at the complete flow rather than treat each platform as a separate technology implementation.

Security is part of the data architecture

Regulatory data programmes also deal with sensitive customer and financial information.

Security and privacy therefore need to be considered as part of the data architecture.

Depending on the use case, controls can include:

  • GDPR
  • PII protection
  • Encryption
  • Role-Based Access Control
  • Data masking
  • Audit logging
  • Data retention
  • Access reviews
  • Vendor controls

For technology resilience, DORA adds another important dimension.

It focuses on digital operational resilience, including ICT risk, incident management, resilience testing and third-party technology risk.

So a modern banking data platform needs to be designed not only for data correctness, but also for security, availability and recoverability.

That is where the role of a Data Engineering Manager becomes important.

The responsibility is not simply to manage individual technology components.

It is to make sure that the end-to-end data flow works as one controlled system.

The delivery challenge

Technology is only one part of the problem.

Large regulatory programmes normally involve many teams: data engineers, solution architects, business analysts, risk specialists, finance teams, compliance, security, infrastructure, testing, operations, third-party vendors and regulatory stakeholders.

Getting all of these teams to deliver against the same outcome is often harder than the technology itself.

In my experience, a few things make a significant difference.

Clear ownership

Who owns the data?

Who owns the control?

Who approves the transformation?

If these questions are not clear, issues can remain open for much longer than they should.

Integrated planning

How do multiple teams deliver against the same regulatory milestone?

A change in one source system can affect the data platform, testing, reporting and ultimately the regulatory submission.

The plan therefore needs to connect those activities rather than treating them as separate workstreams.

Dependency management

Which source system, vendor, interface or downstream platform could affect the delivery?

These dependencies need to be visible early.

Data quality governance

Who investigates an issue?

Who owns the remediation?

Who signs off the resolution?

Without clear accountability, data quality issues can move between teams without being properly resolved.

Testing

Can we prove that the data and calculations are correct?

Testing needs to cover more than application functionality. Data reconciliation, transformation rules, lineage and reporting outputs all need to be considered.

Auditability

Can we reproduce what happened at a particular reporting date?

This becomes particularly important when questions are raised after the reporting cycle has completed.

The role of a data engineering manager

This is where I played a key role across large-scale banking data and regulatory programmes.

As a Data Engineering Manager and SAFe® 5 RTE, my responsibility was not limited to managing engineering teams. I was responsible for connecting regulatory requirements, data architecture, engineering delivery and programme governance into a coordinated delivery model.

The role covered the full data delivery lifecycle:

Regulatory requirement → Business requirement → Data requirement → Architecture → Engineering → Data quality → Lineage → Testing → Reporting → Evidence

I worked across multiple cross-functional teams, including data engineers, architects, business analysts, risk, finance, compliance, testing and operations.

My focus was on making sure that:

  • Data requirements were clearly understood and translated into deliverable engineering work.
  • Source systems, downstream dependencies and data flows were identified and managed.
  • Data pipelines and transformations were delivered with appropriate controls.
  • Data quality issues had clear ownership, impact assessment and remediation plans.
  • Data lineage and traceability were maintained across the reporting chain.
  • Reconciliation was incorporated into the delivery process.
  • Regulatory reporting requirements were supported by reliable and auditable data.
  • Dependencies across multiple Scrum teams were actively managed.
  • Risks, issues and delivery progress were visible through programme governance.
  • Delivery remained aligned with regulatory milestones and business outcomes.

The engineering challenge was only one part of the role.

The bigger challenge was connecting people, processes, data and technology across a complex banking environment.

That meant working across platforms such as Teradata, Informatica, Tableau, Murex 3.0, Athena, SAP and nCino, while ensuring that the resulting data supported regulatory, risk and business requirements.

For me, the role of a Data Engineering Manager in a regulatory programme is therefore about more than delivering data pipelines.

It is about creating confidence that the right data reaches the right place, at the right time, with the right controls and with a clear audit trail.

That is where data engineering moves beyond technology delivery and becomes part of regulatory confidence and business decision-making.

One version of the truth

The phrase "single source of truth" is often used too casually.

In a banking environment, I prefer to think about it as:

One controlled version of the truth for a defined business purpose.

Different systems will continue to exist.

Murex will have its purpose.

SAP will have its purpose.

CRM will have its purpose.

Core banking will have its purpose.

The objective is not necessarily to replace every system.

The objective is to establish controlled data flows, clear ownership, consistent definitions, reliable transformations and traceability across the landscape.

That is a much more realistic approach to large banking data environments.

The bigger lesson

Regulatory compliance is not something that can simply be added at the end of a technology project.

It needs to be considered from the beginning.

The architecture needs to support it.

The data model needs to support it.

The engineering pipelines need to support it.

Security needs to support it.

Testing needs to support it.

Operations needs to support it.

And delivery governance needs to provide evidence that it works.

Regulatory programmes are more than compliance projects. They are enterprise data transformation programmes with regulatory outcomes.

When the data is accurate, traceable, secure and available at the right time, the technology organisation is doing more than helping the bank comply.

It is helping the bank understand its data, support better risk decisions and operate with greater confidence.
