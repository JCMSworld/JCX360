Feedback is useful when it is timely

An observation on feedback, timing and accountability in technology delivery.

Published on October 2, 2026

Feedback is one of the most useful tools in any delivery environment.

When it is timely, specific and connected to the work, it gives people an opportunity to understand what is working, what needs to improve and where expectations may need to change.

But what happens when feedback comes much later?

That is where things become more complicated.

Feedback has a time dimension

During a programme, people are constantly making decisions, managing stakeholders, solving problems and dealing with changing priorities.

If something is not working, timely feedback gives the person an opportunity to respond.

A simple conversation can often resolve an issue before it becomes a bigger problem.

But if the same feedback appears several months later, the situation is different.

People may not remember the specific circumstances.

Priorities may have changed.

The people involved may have moved to different roles.

And the opportunity to correct the behaviour at the time has already passed.

This does not make later feedback invalid.

It does mean that context becomes much more important.

Feedback should not be a surprise

One principle I have learned through delivery is that people should not normally discover a significant performance concern for the first time during a formal review.

If something matters enough to influence an assessment, it is usually better to discuss it when it happens.

That creates an opportunity for:

Expectation → Observation → Feedback → Action → Follow-up

Rather than:

Event → Silence → Months pass → Formal feedback

The second approach makes it much harder for everyone involved.

Who owns the feedback?

There is another interesting question.

If stakeholder feedback is required as part of a performance or delivery review, who owns collecting it?

The individual can certainly ask stakeholders for feedback.

But the manager or organisation responsible for the review also has a role in making sure that the feedback process happens at the right time and that expectations are clear.

Otherwise, there is a risk of putting the individual in a strange position.

They are effectively being asked to create the evidence that will later be used to assess them.

That does not always produce the best outcome.

Feedback should be evidence based

Good feedback should answer some simple questions:

  • What happened?
  • When did it happen?
  • What was expected?
  • What was observed?
  • What was the impact?
  • Was the feedback communicated at the time?
  • What changed afterwards?

Without this context, feedback can easily become subjective.

This is particularly important in complex programmes where different stakeholders may have very different views of the same situation.

One stakeholder may see a delivery decision as effective.

Another may have preferred a different approach.

Neither view automatically represents the complete picture.

The lesson for delivery leaders

For me, the lesson is not that late feedback is always wrong.

Sometimes feedback genuinely becomes available later.

The lesson is that good feedback needs context, timing and accountability.

If something needs to improve, say it early.

If something is working well, recognise it while the contribution is still visible.

If formal feedback is required, make the expectations and ownership clear.

And if feedback arrives much later, understand the circumstances before turning it into a conclusion.

From feedback to learning

Technology delivery is full of situations where the process is only understood properly after experiencing it.

That is one of the reasons I believe experienced delivery leaders should continue reflecting on how things actually work, not just how the process is supposed to work.

Sometimes the biggest learning comes from the uncomfortable questions:

Was the expectation clear?

Was the feedback timely?

Was there an opportunity to respond?

Was the evidence specific?

Who owned the process?

Those questions are useful far beyond performance reviews.

They apply to delivery governance, stakeholder management, programme controls and leadership.

For me, that is the real value of feedback.

It should not simply document what happened. It should help people improve while there is still time to act.

If you really care about the company, the client and the quality of delivery, timely feedback matters.

Whether the feedback is good or bad, positive or negative, success or failure, it has an impact.

Good feedback can reinforce what is working.

Constructive feedback gives people a chance to correct something while there is still time to act.

And difficult feedback, when given early and with the right context, can prevent a small issue from becoming a bigger one.

Once the work is finished and months have passed, the value of feedback can be very different.

The opportunity to act on it may already be gone.

For me, that is why feedback is not just a performance process.

It is part of delivery quality.

Whether the outcome is success or failure, good or bad, beautiful or ugly 😂, timely feedback helps people understand what is happening while they can still do something about it.

“No hype. Just practical thinking, experience and continuous learning”
